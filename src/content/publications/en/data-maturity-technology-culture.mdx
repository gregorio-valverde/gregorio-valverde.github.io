I recently finished **_Fundamentals of Data Engineering_** by Joe Reis and Matt Housley. Among all the ideas in the book, one made me think in particular: the **three stages of data maturity** an organisation can move through.

I think it is a very accurate way of representing something that happens continuously in practice. A company's relationship with data does not change simply because it adopts new tools. Its needs, priorities and expectations about how that information should be used also change.

## From “make it work” to building a solid foundation

In the first stage, **Starting with data**, the objective is usually very direct: start using data and prove that it can create value.

Teams are small, there are many different needs and there is still significant uncertainty about what should be built. In that context, it makes sense to prioritise solutions that work and allow the organisation to move quickly.

The challenge appears when those first solutions begin to grow.

This is where, in my view, one of the most interesting transitions begins: moving from **Starting with data** to **Scaling with data**.

It is no longer enough for a process simply to work. Maintainability, automation, quality, traceability and the ability to add new sources without turning every integration into an isolated development start to matter.

This is where **orchestrated pipelines, more consistent data models, DataOps practices and infrastructure capable of growing** become increasingly relevant. Tools such as Airflow or dbt, together with relational or cloud storage platforms, can become important parts of that professionalisation.

But building the technical layer is only one part of the journey.

## When technology moves faster than the organisation

An organisation can significantly improve its data infrastructure without its culture evolving at the same speed.

We can automate pipelines, improve modelling, introduce tests or centralise information. However, if the people who need to use that data do not understand where it comes from, do not trust it, or continue working exactly as they did before, the impact of all that technical progress will remain limited.

I find this tension particularly relevant because it also changes the role of the Data Engineer.

Our job is not only to move information from one system to another or to build an architecture that is technically correct. As maturity increases, we also need to help **build trust, reduce uncertainty and make data genuinely usable within the organisation's processes**.

That requires communication.

It means explaining why a new model is more reliable, why certain metrics need to be calculated in a specific way, or why automating a process can change the way a business area works.

The platform may be ready before the organisation is. That gap is one of the hardest challenges to solve.

## Leading with data: the change that really matters

The third stage, **Leading with data**, represents something different.

At this point, data is no longer only a support tool. It starts to be treated as a product, ownership becomes clearer, and information becomes part of the way the company makes decisions and builds its processes.

Getting there requires technology, of course, but it also requires trust and adoption.

That is why I do not think data maturity should be measured only by a company's technology stack. Having more tools, more pipelines or a more sophisticated architecture does not automatically make an organisation data-driven.

The real evolution appears when **technical capability and organisational mindset begin to move forward together**.

> Technical evolution is exciting, but the mindset transformation is what really makes the difference.

For me, this is one of the most interesting parts of Data Engineering: building systems that can scale, while also making sure those systems are eventually understood, used and valued by the business.
